# Observing both eclipses on consecutive parts of one orbit

The table windows.csv gives 17 possible pairs of time windows between 2027 August 1 and December 31 (UTC). It covers the expected minima of four fitted histories (Kepler, a nonrepeating trough, cubic, and sinusoid with a free offset), with two additional hours on each side for the approaching and receding parts of the eclipse and a local brightness baseline. A four-hour light curve per eclipse is long enough to span both flanks of the modeled ~90–100 minute FWHM. Forecast uncertainty, stellar shape, and weather can still affect any exposure run.

On October 17 UT, Siding Spring in Australia can observe from 11:06 to 15:25 UT. On October 18 UT, Cerro Tololo in Chile can observe from 02:07 to 06:26 UT, on the same orbital cycle. The Kepler centers are 13:21 UT and 04:13 UT; the predicted heliocentric interval from primary to secondary is 891.7 minutes (14 h 52 min). The single-trough plateau model predicts 919.5 minutes (15 h 20 min), a 27.8-minute difference. The target altitude stays above 43° and the Sun below −33° on the sampled visibility grid. These are candidate observing times only. Exact table times refer to UTC at each site and are rounded to a minute. The paired duration forecast is a separate test of the sense of orbital precession.

Run python windows.py with NumPy, SciPy, pyerfa and Astropy from the delivered layout. The script iterates eclipse phase to convergence and converts HJD (UTC) to site-specific UTC by subtracting the projected Sun-to-observer light travel time. Position checks use unrefracted geocentric ephemerides at 11 samples over each interval. Retention requires the binary above 30° and the Sun below −15°. For these future dates Astropy uses extrapolated Earth rotation, adequate for degree-scale planning. Moonlight, seeing, telescope availability and changes in the timing law are outside this feasibility check.
