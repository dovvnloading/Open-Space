# Physical interpretation of the coherent fits

The 68-pulsar narrowband scan tested 9,688 spatial and temporal templates. It produced 108 clusters of threshold crossings, of which 77 passed the internal leave-one-pulsar-out test. A cluster is local to one waveform and spatial family. The same observations and epoch can therefore appear in several clusters. Each amplitude is the fitted coefficient of the specified waveform after timing-model and noise projection. It can be much larger than the residual excursion present in the data. The alternate waveform at a given epoch is another fit to the same data. The checks in the evidence table determine which independent telescope observations support each fit.

The “largest deletion effect” above denotes the deletion yielding the least significant leave-one-out result. The remaining array still satisfies the internal local threshold after any one deletion. Two or more unusual pulsars can pass this test together. For J1939+2134, NANOGrav has reported multi-year changes in pulse shape attributed to variable scattering. Those measurements identify a reason to test alternative chromatic noise models. They do not establish an origin for each repeated dipole response. [Jacobson-Bell et al., §IV.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.05453v1)

## Checks against source and instrument history

- **MJD 54750–54810 and 57510–57540.** The J1713+0747 timing changes near 54750 and 57510 were already reported and recovered with independent European telescopes. The 2008 NANOGrav pulse-profile peak was measured at 54780. A new appearance of one of these epochs in a tensor or dipole fit calls for checking how the other pulsars respond after the timing fit. [Jacobson-Bell et al., §IV.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.05453v1) · [EPTA timing comparison](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/509/4/5538/6433664)
- **MJD 57090.** This epoch is close to independently reported changes in J0437−4715 and J1643−1224 around 57070 and 57074, respectively, as well as a chromatic cusp in J2043+1711 near 57084.7. Of those three earlier events, the J1643−1224 and J2043+1711 sightlines participated in these fits. J0437−4715 did not. At 57090 J0023+0923 has the largest deletion effect for both the memory and ephemeris ramps. The nearby literature events establish possible pulsar-specific structure. Identifying the source of either array fit would require showing that the predicted angular pattern is present across the other sightlines. [Parkes profile observations](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.06109) · [Hazboun et al., Table 4](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.22597v1)
- **MJD 57810.** The best-fit memory strain is 2.26 × 10⁻¹² at RA 136.04°, Dec −5.98°, with 54 eligible pulsars. The published 15-year memory search found no significant signal. Its sky- and polarization-marginalized 95% limit around this epoch is a few times 10⁻¹⁴, read from Figure 2. The discrepancy in scale weighs against attributing this fit to memory. A direct pointwise exclusion would require limits at the fitted sky direction, polarization and epoch under a comparable noise model. [Agazie et al., Figures 1–4](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.18599)
- **MJD 57930–58410.** NANOGrav removed Arecibo data affected by a faulty local oscillator over 57984–58447. The 57930 step and ramp epochs precede the cut. The steps at 58080 and the broad pulses centered on 58170, 58290 and 58410 fall within it. Thus the contributing sky distribution and observing systems can change sharply near these times. The clock reference failure at Arecibo on 58482–58483 was a separate, documented event. None of the retained peaks is centered on those two dates. [NANOGrav observing cuts](https://doi.org/10.3847/2041-8213/acda9a) · [Arecibo clock record](https://naic.nrao.edu/arecibo/phil/hardware/clocks/aotime_memo_19Jan13.pdf)
- **Other epochs.** For each remaining fit the table records either repeated responses of other templates, sensitivity to observational coverage, or the pulsar with the strongest effect in deletion tests. A clock coefficient requires comparison with terrestrial time standards. A barycentric displacement requires comparison with physical planetary ephemerides. NANOGrav and EPTA both use DE440, so the EPTA check cannot independently eliminate that ephemeris as a shared systematic. [Release time standards and ephemerides](https://doi.org/10.3847/1538-4357/ad5291) · [NANOGrav noise budget](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.16218)
