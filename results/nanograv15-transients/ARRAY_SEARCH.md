# Common transient search

The discovery sample comprises NANOGrav narrowband pulsars with at least 12 residual degrees of freedom. Epochs lie on a 30-day MJD grid. Each pulsar needs six observing days on either side and one day within 180 days on each side for a step or ramp. For Gaussian pulses the latter interval is three Gaussian widths. A test needs at least eight eligible pulsars. A rank-deficient sky response is excluded. The complete trial table gives the number of pulsars at each tested epoch.

| Spatial response | Waveforms | Components of fitted amplitude | Other responses projected out |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clock monopole | Step, ramp, 30- and 120-day Gaussian | Signed timing shift in µs or drift in µs/yr | Three ephemeris dipole components |
| Ephemeris dipole | Same four waveforms | Three timing shifts in equatorial Cartesian coordinates, in µs or µs/yr | Clock monopole |
| Tensor Earth-term memory | Ramp | Plus and cross strain in the local east–north polarization basis | Clock and all three ephemeris components |

The tensor search uses 48 Fibonacci-grid source directions and fits both polarizations continuously. The ramp amplitude for clock and ephemeris signals is its change in one Julian year. Memory amplitude is the Euclidean norm of the two strain steps. A Gaussian width is its standard deviation. Each template is projected through exactly the same timing, instrument, red and dispersion nuisance design and release white-noise transform as the data. Pulsars can contribute with either sign to the ephemeris and tensor responses. Reported positions and epochs are grid fits. A direction and a significance on that grid do not establish an astrophysical source identity.

### False-alarm calculation

Each pulsar contributes its scalar product between projected residuals and a normalized projected template. Their joint covariance includes an additional, fixed stochastic common-process allowance. The model contains three independent Gaussian spatial processes with Hellings–Downs, monopolar, and unit-vector dot-product correlations. Each uses the first 60 harmonic pairs on the union timespan of the primary release, spectral index 13/3 and a strain-equivalent amplitude ceiling of 6 × 10⁻¹⁵ at one inverse Julian year. Each Fourier mode is evaluated at each pulsar's own observation times and projected through its actual timing and nuisance design. The ceiling is a conservative modeling choice, not a measured clock or ephemeris noise amplitude. NANOGrav reported a median GWB amplitude of 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁵ for this index. [Agazie et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.16213)

For each epoch and sky response the amplitude estimator uses fixed linear weights derived from sampling, release errors, nuisance columns and the common covariance ceiling. Each pulsar's residual sum of squares divided by the lower chi-square quantile at 0.0005 divided by the number of analyzed pulsars supplies an upper bound on its unknown independent white variance. The degrees of freedom are the rank of the noise-weighted residual projection. An independent additive Gaussian common process can only reduce the probability that the residual norm is this small. With probability at least 0.9995 all white variances are bounded simultaneously under the model.

The estimator's covariance uses those variance bounds and the full common covariance ceiling. On the simultaneous coverage event this matrix dominates its true covariance. The tail of a chi-square variable with one, three or two degrees of freedom bounds the statistic for clock, ephemeris or tensor fits. The reported global probability bound is the sum of 0.0005 and the local tail times the number of eligible array tests, capped at one. This argument allows overlapping epochs, sky points and event families. It assumes the predeclared Gaussian noise model, release relative errors, valid residual projection and common-spectrum ceiling. Additional narrow spectral lines, heavier tails, clock discontinuities and unmodeled chromatic noise can change the calibration. The individual-pulsar search has its own 0.005 budget. In the array files, `p_global` covers all coherent trials and `p_joint` is twice this value, capped at one, to cover both discovery searches.

All threshold hits are retained in the trials table. Adjacent threshold-crossing epochs within each family and waveform are grouped across sky directions. The search reports the strongest point in each contiguous group and drops each pulsar in turn. Every reduced array must still have a local p below 0.05 for internal survival. A surviving common-pattern candidate remains a candidate if the wideband or external reduction lacks coverage or gives insufficient support. Both comparison tests freeze the discovery waveform, epoch, direction and signed polarization or dipole axis. Every overlapping pulsar that brackets the epoch supplies a local amplitude, uncertainty and predicted amplitude. A coherent comparison additionally requires an eight-pulsar geometry among the overlapping discovery pulsars. The wideband processing check shares observations with narrowband. The external EPTA result is separately labeled.

### Amplitude limits and sensitivity

Every searched epoch and sky entry carries a model-conditional 95% amplitude upper bound. Let K be the total number of eligible array templates, d the number of signal components, V the conservative coefficient covariance and b the fitted vector. A simultaneous confidence ellipsoid uses the chi-square quantile at 1 − (0.05 − 0.0005)/K with d degrees of freedom. The Euclidean norm of b plus the square root of this quantile times the largest eigenvalue of V bounds the true amplitude norm (the square root applies to the whole product). These bounds account for all searched grid points and the estimated white scales. For each family and shape the summary reports the least constraining bound over eligible epochs and sky directions. It applies to a single exact grid waveform under the stated noise model. Empty areas of the grid, mismatched waveforms and astrophysical rate inferences lie outside its coverage. The injection curves separately quantify detection completeness with epoch and sky mismatch.

For each family and temporal shape, 256 synthetic perturbations are injected into the actual compressed primary residuals. Pulsar-specific amplitudes follow the simulated sky response. Each draw picks an eligible grid cell uniformly and offsets the epoch by −12, −6, 0, 6 or 12 days with equal probability. Clock signs, dipole directions, tensor sky positions and polarization orientations are randomized. The trial at the nearest eligible sky grid point at that epoch is scored, with the variance estimates recomputed after each injection. The signal must cross the blind family-wise threshold with aligned fitted amplitude and survive each of the leave-one-out tests. This nearest-cell criterion is a sufficient condition for a bank hit. A different sky point, epoch or waveform could also pass. The resulting recovery fraction measures the specified experiment on this residual realization. It does not give a population-rate or abundance constraint.

The amplitude ladder includes zero and powers of two from 2⁻⁸ to 2¹² µs (or µs/yr, converted to strain for memory). The sensitivity summary gives the first tested amplitude at which a one-sided exact binomial lower bound reaches 90% recovery. The bounds cover the nine family/shape combinations and every amplitude simultaneously at 95% by Bonferroni correction. Null values mark curves that never reach this criterion. External checks characterize candidates and are outside this completeness definition. The curves retain background-only recovery, partial recovery and possible nonmonotonicity from variance re-estimation.

NANOGrav previously searched this data set for gravitational-wave memory. The different noise, template and selection assumptions matter when comparing limits. [Agazie et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.18599)
