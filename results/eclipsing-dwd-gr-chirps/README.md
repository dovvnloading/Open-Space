# Two relativistic clocks in seven eclipsing double white dwarfs

The orbital periods and component masses in van Roestel et al. imply a **12.99-second orbital-decay advance in 10 years for ZTF J0720+6439**, compared with 0.6 seconds in the preprint. Its spectrum provides a separate estimate of the scale. Two other binaries in the sample have first post-Newtonian apsidal periods of **88 and 97 years**, compared with the paper's estimate of 460 years. These are predictions conditional on the published system parameters. An eclipse-timing measurement of either effect in these targets remains open. [Input paper, version 1](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.15580v1)

## Orbital decay

Equation 3 of the preprint prints a factor of $2\pi$ multiplying $(G\mathcal M/P)^{5/3}$. The circular point-mass quadrupole formula for the magnitude has $(2\pi)^{8/3}$ in that position. The ratio is $(2\pi)^{5/3}=21.3944$. The independently recalculated values exceed the seven printed rate magnitudes by factors of 18.25–21.28. These ratios divide a function of two rounded marginal mass estimates by a separately tabulated rate. Individual posterior samples and the authors' calculation were unavailable, so the small variation around the analytic factor cannot be attributed uniquely. [Peters (1964)](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRev.136.B1224) · [Source equations 3 and 6 and table 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.15580v1)

The advance is the magnitude of the quadratic timing residual $\dot P t^2/(2P)$ relative to a linear ephemeris tangent at the initial time. Fitting an epoch and period absorbs part of that displacement. Three independent eclipse times, at 0, 5, and 10 years, retain one quarter of the ten-year advance as the midpoint residual relative to the endpoint chord. If each has 0.5 s independent uncertainty, the contrast uncertainty is $\sqrt{1.5}\,0.5=0.612$ s. Under those particular conditions J0720 gives an expected 5.30-sigma curvature and J1356 gives 1.06 sigma. This calculation assumes stable cycle counts and time standards. The preprint's Table A.2 lists J0720 CHIMERA observations in January 2020 and February 2022. Their precision and sampling must be evaluated from the light curves before claiming a detection. [Observation log and stated precision](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.15580v1)

## Two checks on the decay scale

1. For three systems with double-lined spectroscopy, we used the observed velocity semi-amplitudes in **Table 2**. At the Table 3 inclination, a circular double-lined orbit has $|\dot P|=(192\pi/5)K_1K_2(K_1+K_2)^3/(c\sin i)^5$. The ten-year advance for J0720 is 17.69 s, with 9.15–31.44 s over the quoted velocity corners. For J1356 it is 2.18 s (1.78–2.65 s). The spectroscopy also enters the paper's joint fit, so these are separately computed observables with overlapping input data. The inclination correction is below 0.5% for J0720. [Table 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.15580v1)
2. The same code gives $\dot P=-8.19\times10^{-12}$ s/s for SDSS J0651+2844, whose measured value is $(-9.8\pm2.8)\times10^{-12}$ s/s. Kosakowski et al. independently reported the 3.6±0.1 s ten-year advance for J0221 in 2023, providing a prior numerical check. We make no novelty claim for that target's prediction. [Hermes et al. (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1088/2041-8205/757/2/L21) · [Kosakowski et al. (2023), J0221 section](https://lweb.cfa.harvard.edu/~wbrown/Files/elmss_ii.pdf)

## Apsidal motion

Evaluating the preprint's own equation 4 with Table 3 gives 4.1047°/year (J0238+0933) and 3.6935°/year (J1758+7642). The first post-Newtonian formula $3n(GMn/c^3)^{2/3}/(1-e^2)$ gives 4.1048°/year and 3.6936°/year. We used $e=0$ in the rate alone. At the paper's small-eccentricity scale the omitted correction is of order a few parts per million. The mass-corner ranges are 3.64–4.52 and 2.91–4.44°/year. The calculation describes relativistic precession. Rotation, tidal quadrupoles or third-body perturbations could add to it. [Sections 5.2 and 6](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.15580v1)

Subtracting the tabulated Rømer delays from the measured offsets of the secondary eclipse gives eccentric components of 13.5 and 11.8 s. To first order at high inclination, their evolution is $D(t)=A\cos(\omega_0+\dot\omega t)$ where $A=2Pe/\pi$. A single phase measures $e\cos\omega_0$. Under the source paper's illustrative choice $\omega_0=45°$, the ten-year changes are −12.18 and −9.46 s. Other orientations can make the two-epoch difference zero. [Equations 1–2 and table 3](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.15580v1)

Three epochs give an orientation-independent prediction. With equal spacing $T$ and $u=\cos(\dot\omega T)$, the eccentric offsets obey $D_2-2uD_1+D_0=0$. For a known initial offset, at least one of the two subsequent changes $|D_1-D_0|$, $|D_2-D_0|$ must be at least $2|D_0|(1-u)/(1+2u)$ for $u>0$. This follows by writing $D_2-D_0=2u(D_1-D_0)-2(1-u)D_0$ and applying the triangle inequality. For observations ten and twenty years after the reference measurement, the lower bounds are **2.65 s** for J0238 and **1.82 s** for J1758 at the central inputs. A constant Rømer delay cancels from the changes. A proper timing fit would use the individual dates and covariances, and fit the two quadratures of eccentricity. The published offsets combine several observing seasons and are not individual time-tagged measurements.

## Data, assumptions and reproduction

`parameters.csv` transcribes the period in days, masses in solar units, inclination in degrees, unsigned printed decay rate in units of $10^{-15}$ s/s, and eccentric-source delays in seconds from Table 3. The velocity columns come from Table 2 and are in km/s. An empty field means unused or unavailable. Each `minus` and `plus` is the quoted marginal uncertainty. The mass and velocity envelopes simultaneously vary both inputs to the respective quoted low and high values, holding other inputs fixed. These envelopes describe sensitivity and have no joint confidence-level interpretation. The chirp model uses detached circular point masses, neglects tidal spin-up and line-of-sight acceleration, and treats $P$ as constant in the quadratic coefficient. The binary-component mass covariance and a precise ephemeris covariance would be needed for a predictive posterior.

Run `python reproduce.py --check` in this directory to verify the four saved outputs using the Python standard library. Running `python reproduce.py` regenerates them. Two implementations obtain the decay rate independently, one using the mass-period relation and one using energy balance and Kepler's law. The code similarly checks the precession against the preprint's numerical equation. The inputs use nominal $GM_\odot=1.3271244\times10^{20}$ m³/s², exact $c$, and Julian years of 365.25 days.

The scope check on 2026-11-15 used the arXiv submission history, the author’s publication link, Crossref and OpenAlex title searches, and searches for J0720, J0238 and J1758 with orbital decay, apsidal precession and corrigendum. These identified v1 (21 May 2025). We found the prior J0221 prediction and the subsequent population study including these stars.
